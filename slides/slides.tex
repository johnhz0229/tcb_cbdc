      \vspace{0.6em}
      \hl{This is the first blockage: money is created, but it is not spent.}

          \node[font=\scriptsize, color=warm, anchor=north west] at (axis cs:2012.6,-0.55) {lowest rate reached: $-0.50\%$};
          \node[font=\scriptsize, color=quiet, anchor=south west] at (axis cs:2014.5,0.1) {at or below zero, 2014--22};
        \end{axis}
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \small
      The ECB cut its deposit facility rate below zero in June 2014 and kept it at or below zero until July 2022, but it never went lower than $-0.50\%$.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      The reason is the outside option of cash. Banknotes pay zero, so if banks charged savers more than the cost of storing and insuring cash, savers would withdraw their money.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      The lower bound is therefore not exactly zero but minus the cost of holding cash: $\;i \ge -c$.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      \hl{This is the second blockage: interest rates cannot fall far enough to make people spend.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \source{Source: ECB key interest rates.}
\end{frame}

    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \small
      In 2020 Chinese cities distributed digital coupons through mobile payment platforms that had to be used within about a week. Each yuan of subsidy generated roughly three yuan of additional spending.

      \vspace{0.6em}

      \vspace{0.6em}
      \hl{A deadline turns money that would have been saved into spending today.}
    \end{column}

\begin{frame}{A programmable digital euro could build this pressure into money itself, in one of two ways}{A tempting fix}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \begin{axis}[quietaxis, width=\textwidth, height=5.4cm, xmin=0, xmax=40, ymin=0, ymax=1.15,
            xlabel={days}, ylabel={value of a \euro 1 balance}, xtick={0,30}, xticklabels={0,$T$}, ytick={0,1},
            legend style={at={(0.03,0.04)}, anchor=south west}]
          \addplot[warm] coordinates {(0,1) (30,1) (30,0) (40,0)};
          \addlegendentry{expiry date}
          \addplot[accent, domain=0:40, samples=60] {exp(-0.03*x)};
          \addlegendentry{continuous decay}
        \end{axis}
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \small
      A central bank digital currency is programmable, so the central bank could charge holders for keeping money idle.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      With an \textcolor{warm}{expiry date}, nothing happens until day $T$, and then the whole balance is lost.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      With \textcolor{accent}{continuous decay}, also called demurrage, the balance shrinks by a small fraction every day, like Silvio Gesell's stamped money of the 1930s.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      Both designs charge for holding money; they differ only in \emph{when} the charge falls.
      \hl{Which one should a central bank choose?}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{To compare the two designs, consider a holder who must search for good uses of money}{The model}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \node[box] (h) at (0,0) {holder of \euro 1\\[-0.1em]\scriptsize\color{quiet}worth $V$};
        \node[box, draw=accent] (g) at (4.2,1.3) {good purchase\\[-0.1em]\scriptsize\color{quiet}worth $1$};
        \node[box, draw=warm] (b) at (4.2,-1.3) {fire sale\\[-0.1em]\scriptsize\color{quiet}worth $\phi<1$};
        \draw[arr, accent] (h.north east) -- (g.west);
        \draw[arr, warm, dashed] (h.south east) -- (b.west);
        \node[font=\scriptsize, color=accent, align=center] at (1.9,1.25) {search at\\speed $\kappa$};
        \node[font=\scriptsize, color=warm, align=center] at (1.9,-1.25) {always\\possible};
        \node[font=\scriptsize, color=quiet] at (0,-0.85) {cost $\kappa^2/2a$ per day};
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \small
      Each euro is worth $V$ to its holder. Finding a good use for it takes effort: searching at speed $\kappa$ costs $\kappa^2/2a$ per day, and $\kappa$ is the velocity of money. A bad use, such as buying something of little value or selling the token at a discount, is always available and worth $\phi<1$.

      \vspace{0.5em}
      \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
        \textcolor{accent}{Decay at rate $\lambda$:} & $(r+\lambda)\,V = \tfrac a2(1-V)^2$ \\[0.3em]
        \textcolor{warm}{Expiry at $T$:} & $\dfrac{dV}{d\tau} = -rV + \tfrac a2(1-V)^2,\;\; V=\phi$ at $T$
      \end{tabular}

      \vspace{0.5em}
      In both cases the holder spends at speed $\kappa^* = a\,(1-V)$.
      \hl{The less money is worth holding, the faster it moves.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

  \vspace{-0.2cm}
  \small
  Both designs are calibrated to the same average velocity over 30 days. \end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Continuous decay keeps every euro equal, and it is nothing more than a negative interest rate}{Result 2}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \begin{axis}[quietaxis, width=\textwidth, height=5.4cm, xmin=0, xmax=5.2, ymin=1, ymax=1.55,
            xlabel={decay rate $\lambda$ (\% a year)}, ylabel={velocity relative to $\lambda=0$},
            ytick={1,1.1,1.2,1.3,1.4,1.5}, yticklabels={$\times$1.0,$\times$1.1,$\times$1.2,$\times$1.3,$\times$1.4,$\times$1.5},
            title={Velocity rises with the square root of the rate}]
          \addplot[accent, domain=0:5, samples=50] {sqrt((4+x)/4)};
          \addplot[only marks, mark=*, mark size=1.7pt, accent] coordinates {(1,1.12) (2,1.22) (3,1.32)};
          \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=north west] at (axis cs:1,1.12) {$+12\%$};
          \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=north west] at (axis cs:2,1.22) {$+22\%$};
          \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=north west] at (axis cs:3,1.32) {$+32\%$};
        \end{axis}
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \small
      Decay avoids every problem of the expiry date. There is no deadline and no fire sale, every euro has the same value whatever its age, and it can apply to all money rather than to a separate voucher.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      A decay rate $\lambda$ is simply an interest rate of $-\lambda$. Velocity follows $\kappa^* \approx \sqrt{2a\,(r+\lambda)}$, the same square-root rule as in the Baumol--Tobin model of money demand: a decay of 1\% a year raises velocity by about 12\%.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      \hl{Decay addresses both blockages: money moves faster, and a negative rate finally reaches retail balances.} An expiry date belongs to a voucher; decay can belong to money.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A zero-rate digital euro is cash without storage costs, so it pushes the lower bound back up to zero}{The twist}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tikzpicture}[x=5.6cm]
        \draw[hair, line width=0.8pt] (-0.72,0) -- (0.40,0);
        \foreach \x/\l in {-0.5/{$-0.5\%$}, 0/{$0$}} \draw[quiet] (\x,0.08) -- (\x,-0.08) node[below, font=\scriptsize, color=quiet] {\l};
        \node[font=\scriptsize, color=quiet, anchor=west] at (0.18,-0.25) {deposit rate};
        \fill[warm] (-0.5,0) circle (2.8pt);
        \node[above, font=\scriptsize, color=warm, align=center] at (-0.5,0.15) {floor with cash:\\$-c$};
        \fill[accent] (0,0) circle (2.8pt);
        \node[above, font=\scriptsize, color=accent, align=center] at (0,0.15) {floor with a zero-rate\\digital euro: $0$};
        \draw[arr, accent] (-0.47,-0.62) -- (-0.03,-0.62);
        \node[below, font=\scriptsize, color=accent] at (-0.25,-0.68) {the lower bound moves up};
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \small
      A bank cannot pay depositors less than their best alternative in central-bank money, net of the cost of holding it:
      \[
        r_D \;\ge\; \max_j\,\{\, i_j - c_j \,\}.
      \]
      Cash pays zero but costs something to store and insure, so the floor lies slightly below zero. This is why the ECB could reach $-0.50\%$.

      \vspace{0.5em}
      A zero-rate digital euro pays the same zero but costs nothing to hold. \hl{Up to the holding limit, it lifts the floor for deposit rates back to exactly zero}, and takes away the little room below zero that the ECB had.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The lower bound and disintermediation are the same problem: a central-bank rival to bank deposits}{The twist}
  \vspace{0.3cm}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \node[box, minimum width=3.2cm] (d) {bank deposit\\[-0.1em]\scriptsize\color{quiet}pays $r_D$};
    \node[box, minimum width=3.2cm, left=2.6cm of d, draw=warm] (c) {cash\\[-0.1em]\scriptsize\color{quiet}pays $0$, costs $c$ to hold};
    \node[box, minimum width=3.2cm, right=2.6cm of d, draw=accent] (e) {zero-rate digital euro\\[-0.1em]\scriptsize\color{quiet}pays $0$, costs nothing};
    \draw[arr, warm] (d.west) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize] {if $r_D < -c$} node[below, font=\scriptsize\scshape] {lower bound} (c.east);
    \draw[arr, accent] (d.east) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize] {if $r_D < 0$} node[below, font=\scriptsize\scshape] {disintermediation} (e.west);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.5cm}
  \small
  The lower bound and bank disintermediation are usually discussed as separate issues. They are the same mechanism: whenever central-bank money offers a better net return than a bank deposit, depositors move.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  A zero-rate digital euro therefore puts the most pressure on bank deposits precisely when rates are negative, which is when banks' margins are already squeezed. \hl{Fixing its rate at zero ties the digital euro's financial-stability risk to the state of monetary policy.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When costs have a cliff and benefits do not, price and quantity together beat either one alone}{The principle}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \begin{axis}[quietaxis, width=\textwidth, height=5.6cm, xmin=0, xmax=10, ymin=0, ymax=7,

    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \small
      Weitzman (1974) showed that under uncertainty a price works better when the benefit curve is steeper than the cost curve, and a quantity works better when the cost curve is steeper:
      \[
        \Delta_{\text{price} - \text{quantity}} = \frac{\sigma^2\,(b-c)}{2\,b^2}.
      \]
      The marginal cost of deposit outflows has three segments: zero while banks can absorb them, a constant $e$ while banks replace deposits with dearer funding, and vertical once their liquidity buffers run out.

      \vspace{0.4em}
      In normal times demand crosses the flat part, where a price works best. In a crisis it shifts out and hits the cliff, where only a quantity works. \hl{Both happen on the same curve, so the best instrument is a tiered price with a cap} (Roberts and Spence 1976).
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The digital euro should bear interest: zero for payments, decay above, and a cap that moves with the cycle}{Conclusion}
  \vspace{0.2cm}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \setlength\itemsep{0.8em}
    \item Deadlines get money spent, but an expiry date ends in a fire sale and makes one euro worth more than another. It belongs to a voucher, not to money.
    \item Continuous decay keeps every euro equal and is simply a negative interest rate. It is the only form in which money itself can carry a holding cost.
    \item A digital euro fixed at zero is cash without storage costs. It raises the lower bound and creates the most pressure on bank deposits when rates are negative.
    \item The best design combines price and quantity: a free tier of about \euro 500 for payments, decay of about 2.4\% above it, and a cap tied to banks' liquidity.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{0.6cm}
  \begin{center}
    \hl{\itshape Article 16(8) should be replaced by a rule that lets the digital euro bear interest in this form.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Expiry versus decay: derivation and results}{Backup}
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{Holder's problem.} Per unit of balance, with decay $\lambda$:
  \[
    (r+\lambda)V = \max_{\kappa}\Bigl\{\kappa(1-V) - \frac{\kappa^2}{2a}\Bigr\}
    \;\Rightarrow\; \kappa^*=a(1-V),\quad (r+\lambda)V=\tfrac a2(1-V)^2 .
  \]
  \textbf{Square-root rule.} With $x=1-V$ small, $(r+\lambda)(1-x)=\tfrac a2 x^2$, so $x\approx\sqrt{2(r+\lambda)/a}$ and $\kappa^*\approx\sqrt{2a(r+\lambda)}$.

  \textbf{Expiry.} With $\tau=T-t$ left: $dV/d\tau=-rV+\tfrac a2(1-V)^2$, $V(0)=\phi$. If $\tfrac a2(1-\phi)^2>r\phi$, $V>\phi$ before $T$, so the fire sale happens only at $T$.
  Velocity rises to $a(1-\phi)$: finite, but the unspent balance is sold at once. Decay $\lambda$ is set to match the average expiry velocity over $[0,T]$.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
    $T$ & useful (expiry) & dumped & value range & $\lambda$ p.a. & useful (decay) & decayed \\
    \arrayrulecolor{hair}\midrule
    7 d  & 45\% & 55\% & 0.50--0.63 & 1172\% & 41\% & 15\% \\
    30 d & 84\% & 16\% & 0.50--0.80 & 489\%  & 73\% & 16\% \\
    90 d & 97\% & 3\%  & 0.50--0.91 & 161\%  & 88\% & 10\% \\
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Matching a voucher needs decay of hundreds of percent a year; policy-sized decay of 1--3\% raises velocity by 12--32\%. A digital euro can carry a rate, not a voucher.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Prices versus quantities: derivation}{Backup}
  \footnotesize
  Benefit $MB(q)=b_0+\theta-bq$, cost $MC(q)=c_0+cq$, shock $\theta$ with variance $\sigma^2$; optimum $q^*(\theta)=(b_0+\theta-c_0)/(b+c)$.
  \begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0.4em}
    \item Quantity fixed at $\mathbb E q^*$: expected loss $\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2(b+c)}$
    \item Price fixed: $q$ moves by $\theta/b$, expected loss $\dfrac{\sigma^2 c^2}{2b^2(b+c)}$
    \item Difference: $\Delta=\dfrac{\sigma^2(b-c)}{2b^2}$. Prices win if and only if $b>c$.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{Three-segment cost.} $MC(X)=0$ for $X\le X_0$, $e$ for $X_0<X\le L$, $\infty$ beyond $L$.
  The planner's optimum is decentralised by a zero rate up to $M_1$, the rate $-e$ above it, and a cap at $\bar M$:
  \[
    N\,\mathbb E[\min(t,M_1)]=X_0,\qquad i_2=-e,\qquad N\,\mathbb E[\min(d,\bar M)]=\theta D .
  \]
  Under the rate $-e$, Baumol--Tobin demand for tier-2 balances shrinks by $K=\sqrt{r_D/(r_D+e)}=0.31$.
\end{frame}
