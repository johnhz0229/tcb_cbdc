{\LARGE\bfseries\color{accent} Use It or Lose It?}\par
\smallskip
{\large Programmable money and the digital euro's interest rate}\par
\smallskip
{\color{muted} Speaker script \textperiodcentered{} 20 minutes + 10 minutes Q\&A}

\bigskip
\textbf{The story in twelve sentences} \muted{(the slide titles)}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item Use it or lose it?
  \item Money with an expiry date gets spent, and fast.
  \item A digital euro could build the same pressure into money itself, by an expiry date or by continuous decay.
  \item To compare them, take a holder who searches for good uses of money and can always dump it at a loss.
  \item An expiry date pushes out more spending, but ends in a fire sale and splits money into vintages.
  \item Continuous decay keeps every euro equal, and it is nothing more than a negative interest rate.
  \item As a rate, decay is far too weak to replicate a voucher: money can carry a rate, not a stimulus programme.
  \item So the real question is the digital euro's interest rate, and the draft regulation fixes it at zero.
  \item A zero-rate digital euro is cash without storage costs, which lifts the lower bound back to zero.
  \item A negative digital-euro rate can win that space back, but no more while cash still exists.
  \item The best design keeps zero as the default and lets balances above a threshold decay when policy rates turn negative.
  \item The digital euro should be able to bear interest: only as decay, and only when rates are negative.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Numbers to know}
\begin{itemize}
  \item China coupons: about \textyen 3 of extra spending per \textyen 1 of subsidy. France: one-month MPC 61\% with a three-week expiry, 23\% without.
  \item Art.\ 16(8): ``The digital euro shall not bear interest.'' Holding limit under discussion: \euro 1{,}500--3{,}000.
  \item ECB deposit facility rate: $-0.50\%$, September 2019 to July 2022.
  \item 30-day comparison: useful spending 84\% (expiry) vs 73\% (decay); 16\% dumped at the deadline.
  \item Decay needed to match a voucher: 160\%--1{,}200\% a year. A 1\% decay raises velocity by about 12\%.
\end{itemize}

% ================= SLIDES =================
\slidepage{1}{0:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Governments have discovered that if you give people money with a deadline, they spend it.''
  \item ``Today I ask whether a central bank should build that idea into money itself, and what it means for the digital euro.''
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{2}{1:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item In 2020, Chinese cities handed out digital coupons through payment apps, valid for about a week. Each yuan of subsidy produced about three yuan of extra spending.
  \item In France, a randomised experiment gave households prepaid cards. With a three-week expiry, 61\% was spent within a month; without one, 23\%.
  \item ``The deadline is what turns a transfer that would be saved into spending today.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Weren't the Chinese coupons driven by minimum-spend thresholds, not expiry?}
  \A Partly. Ding et al.\ point to the thresholds. That is why I show the French experiment, which isolates the deadline.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{3}{1:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item In a liquidity trap, the central bank adds money and people sit on it; velocity falls.
  \item A programmable digital euro could charge people for sitting on money, in two ways.
  \item An expiry date: nothing happens until day $T$, then the money is gone.
  \item Continuous decay, what Gesell called demurrage: the balance shrinks a little every day.
  \item ``Same idea, different timing. Which should a central bank use?''
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{4}{2:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item A deliberately simple model. Each unit of money is worth $V$ to the holder. A good purchase is worth 1, but finding it takes effort.
  \item The search speed $\kappa$ is the velocity; it costs $\kappa^2/2a$.
  \item There is always a bad option: junk goods, or selling the token at a discount, worth $\phi$.
  \item With an expiry date, value depends on time left and ends at $\phi$. With decay, the decay rate simply adds to the discount rate.
  \item ``In both cases, the less money is worth holding, the faster it is spent.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Why a quadratic search cost?}
  \A It is the simplest convex cost with an interior solution. With linear cost, velocity would literally explode at the deadline; the convex case is the conservative one.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{5}{2:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item I tune the decay rate so both schemes have the same average velocity over 30 days.
  \item Left: under expiry, spending accelerates into the deadline; under decay it is flat.
  \item The expiry date actually gets more useful spending done: 84\% against 73\%. It forces the whole balance out.
  \item But 16\% is still unspent on the last day and is dumped on junk.
  \item Right: an expiring token's value depends on its age. A fresh one is worth 0.8, an old one 0.5. Two euros are no longer the same euro.
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Why match average velocity?}
  \A Velocity is the target in a liquidity trap. Matching total spending is impossible here: decay never reaches the expiry level, which is itself informative.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{6}{2:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Put the two side by side. Expiry ends in a fire sale, prices each unit by its age, and so breaks the rule that one euro equals one euro.
  \item It also cannot apply to all money; it needs a separate vintage for every payment.
  \item Decay has none of these problems. And decay at rate $\lambda$ is exactly an interest rate of $-\lambda$.
  \item Velocity responds with the square root of the holding cost, the same rule as Baumol--Tobin money demand.
  \item ``An expiry date is a feature of a spending programme. Decay can be a feature of money.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Couldn't a smart contract track the vintages?}
  \A Technically yes. But then every wallet holds euros of different value, and shops must either take them at par, which invites arbitrage, or at a discount, which ends the singleness of money.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{7}{1:45}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item How strong would decay need to be to act like a voucher? Between 160\% and 1{,}200\% a year.
  \item Policy-sized negative rates, half a percent to three percent, raise velocity by 6 to 32\%.
  \item Vouchers also rely on things a currency cannot have permanently: new money, a salient deadline, spending thresholds.
  \item ``So money cannot be a stimulus programme. What it can carry is an interest rate.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Don't these numbers depend on your parameters?}
  \A The levels do; the order of magnitude does not. Across the sensitivity grid the matching rate stays in the hundreds of percent (backup B3).
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{8}{1:15}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``That brings us to the digital euro, whose rate is being decided right now.''
  \item The Commission's draft settles it in one sentence: the digital euro shall not bear interest. Not positive, not negative, ever.
  \item It comes with a holding limit, around \euro 1{,}500 to 3{,}000 per person. Council and Parliament are negotiating the final text.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{9}{2:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Depositors can always leave the bank for central-bank money. So a bank cannot pay less than the best such option, net of what it costs to hold it.
  \item Cash pays zero but is costly to store and insure. That is why the ECB could go to $-0.50\%$ in 2019--22 without people emptying their accounts.
  \item A zero-rate digital euro is cash without those costs. Up to the limit, it puts the floor back at exactly zero.
  \item And the outflow comes precisely when negative rates are already squeezing bank margins.
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Retail deposit rates never went negative anyway.}
  \A For small balances, banks chose not to charge. Larger and corporate deposits did pay negative rates. A zero-rate digital euro turns that choice into a legal floor for everyone up to the limit.
  \Q{What about stablecoins?}
  \A Under MiCA, e-money tokens cannot pay interest either. They are another zero-rate option, but with issuer risk.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{10}{2:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item If the digital euro could decay, the floor becomes the higher of $-\lambda$ and the cash floor.
  \item So decay wins back the space that the zero rate gave away, down to $-c$.
  \item But not further: below $-c$, people simply switch to banknotes.
  \item Going deeper would need cash itself to be repriced, for instance Agarwal and Kimball's crawling exchange rate between cash and reserves.
  \item ``A flexible rate protects the policy space we have. It cannot create more.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{How large is $c$?}
  \A It is not estimated here. The 2019--22 episode shows it is at least as large as the negative rates passed on to large depositors without a run into cash.
  \Q{Isn't this just Bordo and Levin (2017)?}
  \A They show an interest-bearing CBDC could remove the lower bound. With cash kept, as in the euro area, a digital-euro rate alone can only restore the cash floor, not remove it.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{11}{2:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item The design follows directly. In normal times: zero, as in the draft. Simple and trusted.
  \item Only when the policy rate is negative do balances above a threshold $M_1$ decay, at the policy rate, but never below the cost of holding cash.
  \item Small balances never decay, so households and everyday payments are untouched.
  \item Decay, never expiry, so every euro stays equal to every other.
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Who would hold a digital euro that can shrink?}
  \A Nobody's first $M_1$ ever shrinks. Above it, the charge equals what a large bank deposit would pay; the digital euro is no worse than a bank account, it just stops being better.
  \Q{The ECB says the digital euro will not be programmable money.}
  \A That means no restrictions on what it can buy. Decay restricts nothing; it is a rate on a balance, like interest on reserves.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{12}{1:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the four points.
  \item Close: ``Deadlines belong to spending programmes. Money can only carry a rate. The digital euro should be allowed to carry one: zero in normal times, decay when rates are negative, and never an expiry date.''
\end{itemize}

% ================= Q&A + GLOSSARY =================
\clearpage
{\large\bfseries\color{accent} More questions}\par\medskip
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.5em]
  \Q{After the 2022 inflation, why worry about negative rates?}
  \A The lower bound bound for most of 2009--2021. A regulation lasts decades; it should not be designed for the current regime only.
  \Q{Your model is partial equilibrium.}
  \A Yes. The qualitative results, that expiry breaks fungibility and that decay is a rate, do not depend on general equilibrium.
  \Q{Do coupons just pull spending forward?}
  \A Evidence is mixed: Xing et al.\ (2023) find purchases moved forward, Ding et al.\ (2024) do not. The argument here does not rely on it.
  \Q{W\"orgl's stamp scrip was banned. Why would demurrage be acceptable now?}
  \A It was banned because a town issued money, not because demurrage failed. Here the issuer is the central bank and the charge is a policy rate.
  \Q{Does decay hurt the poor?}
  \A Balances below $M_1$ are exempt, which covers most low-income holdings.
  \Q{Is this within the ECB's mandate?}
  \A Setting the rate on its own liabilities is standard monetary policy; Art.\ 16(8) would need a conditional clause instead of a blanket ban.
\end{itemize}
