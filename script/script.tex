\kicker{The story in ten sentences}\par\medskip
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0.45em]
  \item[\quiet{1}] Use it or lose it?
  \item[\quiet{2}] Monetary policy stalls when money is hoarded and rates cannot fall below zero.
  \item[\quiet{3}] Vouchers with a deadline get money spent, and a programmable digital euro could become one.
  \item[\quiet{4}] But an expiry date pushes out more spending, ends in a fire sale and splits money into vintages.
  \item[\quiet{5}] Continuous decay keeps every euro equal: it is simply a negative rate that reaches every wallet.
  \item[\quiet{6}] Yet the digital euro is set at zero, which makes it cash without storage costs and raises the lower bound.
  \item[\quiet{7}] A hard holding limit stops runs in a crisis, but blocks ordinary users in normal times.
  \item[\quiet{8}] When costs have a cliff and benefits do not, price and quantity together beat either alone.
  \item[\quiet{9}] The optimal digital euro has three numbers: a free tier, a Pigouvian rate above it, and a cap.
  \item[\quiet{10}] The digital euro should bear interest: zero for payments, decay above, and a cap that moves with the cycle.
\end{enumerate}

% ================= SLIDES =================
\slidepage{1}{0:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Give people money with a deadline, and they spend it. Today I ask what happens if a central bank builds that idea into money itself, and what it means for the digital euro.''
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{2}{2:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Two things went wrong with monetary policy in the last decade.
  \item First, money was created but not spent. The Eurosystem balance sheet reached \euro 8.8 trillion, yet inflation averaged 0.3\% in 2014--16. Velocity fell.
  \item Second, rates could not go far below zero. For eight years the deposit rate was at or below zero, but never below $-0.50\%$, because savers can always switch to cash, which pays zero.
  \item ``So: money is not spent, and rates cannot fall. Keep both in mind; I will come back to the second one.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Inflation is now above target. Why does this matter?}
  \A The lower bound bound for most of 2009--2021. The digital-euro regulation will last decades and should work in both regimes.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{3}{1:45}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Governments found a way around the first problem: vouchers with a deadline.
  \item In 2020, Chinese cities gave out digital coupons, valid for about a week. Each yuan of subsidy produced about three yuan of extra spending.
  \item A randomised trial in France: with a three-week expiry, 61\% of the transfer was spent within a month; without, 23\%.
  \item ``A digital euro is programmable. So why not give money itself a deadline?''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Weren't the Chinese results driven by minimum-spend thresholds?}
  \A Partly, as Ding et al.\ show. The French trial isolates the deadline.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{4}{2:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item To see what an expiry date does, take a holder who must search for good uses of money. Searching faster is costly. A bad use, junk or a discounted sale, is always available.
  \item The key result: the less money is worth holding, the faster it is spent.
  \item Compare an expiry date with continuous decay, tuned to the same average velocity over 30 days.
  \item Expiry actually gets more useful spending done: 84\% against 73\%. It forces the whole balance out.
  \item But look at the last day: 16\% of issuance is still unspent and goes to junk. And on the right, an expiring euro is worth 0.8 when new and 0.5 when old. One euro is no longer one euro.
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Why a quadratic search cost?}
  \A It is the simplest convex cost. With a linear cost, velocity would literally explode at the deadline; convex is the conservative case.
  \Q{Why match average velocity?}
  \A Velocity is the target in a liquidity trap. Matching total spending is impossible here, because decay always burns part of the balance.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{5}{2:15}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Decay has none of these problems: no fire sale, every unit worth the same, and it can apply to all money.
  \item And decay at rate $\lambda$ is exactly an interest rate of $-\lambda$.
  \item Velocity responds with the square root of the holding cost, the same rule as Baumol--Tobin money demand. One percent of decay raises velocity by about 12\%.
  \item ``So decay unblocks both channels from slide 2: money moves faster, and a negative rate finally reaches retail balances.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Couldn't a smart contract track the vintages of expiring money?}
  \A Yes, but then shops must take old and new euros either at par, which invites arbitrage, or at different prices, which ends the singleness of money.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{6}{2:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item But the draft regulation goes the other way. Art.\ 16(8): the digital euro shall not bear interest. Not positive, not negative.
  \item Why does that matter? A bank cannot pay depositors less than their best central-bank alternative, net of holding costs.
  \item Cash pays zero but is costly to store and insure, so the floor sits a bit below zero. That is why $-0.50\%$ was possible.
  \item A zero-rate digital euro is cash without those costs. It pushes the floor back up to exactly zero.
  \item ``The lower bound and disintermediation are the same problem: a central-bank rival to bank deposits.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Retail deposit rates never went negative anyway.}
  \A For small balances banks chose not to charge. Large and corporate deposits did pay negative rates. A zero-rate digital euro makes zero a legal floor for everyone, up to the limit.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{7}{2:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item The ECB's answer to the disintermediation risk is a hard cap, around \euro 3{,}000.
  \item In normal times people hold the digital euro to pay. 18\% would like to hold more than \euro 3{,}000; the cap simply blocks them. A price would let them hold more if they pay for it.
  \item In a crisis people run for safety. Without a cap, my calibration gives an outflow of \euro 4.7 trillion; with \euro 3{,}000 it is about \euro 0.65 trillion. A rate gap of two percent means nothing next to the fear of losing a deposit.
  \item ``So price works in normal times, and only quantity works in a crisis.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Where do the outflow numbers come from?}
  \A The ECB published \euro 156bn at a \euro 500 cap and \euro 699bn at \euro 3{,}000. I fit the survey distribution of deposits to those two points; the implied population is 360 million, the actual euro-area population. Without a cap, the model gives \euro 4.7tn, close to Meller and Soons' ``almost \euro 5 trillion''.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{8}{2:30}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item This is an old question in economics: prices or quantities? Weitzman's answer depends on slopes.
  \item If the benefit curve is steeper than the cost curve, use a price. If the cost curve is steeper, use a quantity.
  \item Here the cost of deposit outflows has three segments: free while banks absorb it, a constant cost $e$ while they replace funding, and a cliff once liquidity buffers are gone.
  \item In normal times the benefit curve crosses the flat part: price. In a crisis it shifts out and hits the cliff: quantity.
  \item ``Both happen on one curve, so the answer is both: a tiered rate with a cap, as Roberts and Spence showed.''
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Isn't the ECB already proposing tiering?}
  \A Bindseil (2020) proposed a two-tier rate. What I add is the reason, from the shape of the cost curve, and a calibration of each tier.
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{9}{3:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Each segment of the cost curve gives one number.
  \item Free tier: payments should only use what banks absorb at no cost. The ECB expects digitalisation to bring \euro 127bn into deposits; spending exactly that gives a free tier of about \euro 510.
  \item Above the tier, the rate is minus the bank's cost of replacing deposit funding: the refinancing rate minus the deposit rate, about $-2.4\%$.
  \item The cap keeps crisis outflows within what banks can bear; at today's tolerance that is \euro 3{,}000.
  \item The table: the cap alone blocks 18\% of users and leaks \euro 648bn when rates are negative. Price alone fails in a crisis: \euro 4.7 trillion. The hybrid blocks 7\%, leaks \euro 158bn, and holds in a crisis.
\end{itemize}
\asked
\begin{itemize}
  \Q{Is $-2.4\%$ not a huge penalty?}
  \A In normal times almost nobody pays it; it only stops the digital euro from becoming a savings account. And it is an upper bound: if central-bank funding replaces deposits cheaply, the social cost is lower.
  \Q{Why \euro 127bn for the free tier?}
  \A It leaves banks no worse off than today. The sensitivity: \euro 100bn to \euro 300bn gives a tier between \euro 370 and \euro 1{,}840 (backup).
\end{itemize}

\slidepage{10}{1:00}
\say
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the four points.
  \item On the chart: the cap depends on how much liquidity banks have. When buffers are thick, the cap can rise; when they thin, it should fall. A countercyclical cap.
  \item Close: ``Deadlines belong to vouchers. Money can only carry a rate. The digital euro should be allowed to carry one: zero for payments, decay above, and a cap that moves with the cycle.''
\end{itemize}

% ================= Q&A + GLOSSARY =================
\clearpage
\kicker{More questions}\par\medskip
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.6em]
  \Q{Isn't negative interest on CBDC already in Bordo and Levin (2017)?}
  \A Yes, as an idea. The new parts here are why it must be decay rather than expiry, and how a rate and a cap should be combined and calibrated.
  \Q{Would people accept a digital euro that can shrink?}
  \A Balances up to the free tier never shrink. Above it, the charge only makes the digital euro no better than a bank account.
  \Q{The ECB says the digital euro will not be programmable money.}
  \A That means no restrictions on what it buys. Decay restricts nothing; it is a rate on a balance.
  \Q{Doesn't a countercyclical cap signal panic when it is lowered?}
  \A It should move by rule with published liquidity data, not by discretion, so that it is anticipated rather than read as a signal.
  \Q{Your model is partial equilibrium.}
  \A Yes. The qualitative results do not depend on general equilibrium; the magnitudes are calibrated to the ECB's own numbers.
\end{itemize}
